While the focus for many investors is on selecting promising stocks and timing their market entries, experienced traders know that managing downside risk can make the difference between long-term success and failure. Thus, protecting your holdings from significant losses is just as crucial as identifying profitable trades.
Stop-loss orders
are among the best risk management tools, automating trades that limit potential losses and protect your money. When used well, a stop-loss order can make a world of difference. And just about any investor can benefit from this tool.
What Is a Stop-Loss Order?
A stop-loss order is a trading instruction given to a broker to automatically sell a security when it reaches a specific price point.
It acts
as a safety net
that catches you before a falling stock price can significantly impact your portfolio. Stop-loss orders are, in this way, like having a full-time assistant watching your investments—they execute automatically when certain conditions are met, removing emotion from the equation.
The mechanics of a stop-loss order are fairly straightforward: When you place a stop-loss order (it's straightforward on most broker's platforms), you're saying, "If this stock falls to $X price, sell my position." The trigger or stop price can be set at any level below the present market price.
For instance, if you bought shares at $100 and want to limit your potential loss to 10%, you would set your stop-loss order at $90. If the stock drops to or below $90, the order automatically converts to a
market sell order
, getting you out of the position before further losses occur.
Tip
Stop-loss orders remove emotion from the equation—a crucial advantage given that
behavioral finance
research has long shown that investors typically hold losing positions too long while selling winners too early—the so-called "disposition effect." Fortunately, the same research shows that stop-losses effectively nullify this tendency.
Stop-Losses Keep You From Tipping Your Hand
Unlike limit orders visible to other market participants, stop-loss orders typically reside on your broker's books until triggered, making them a discreet way to protect your investment.
This aspect is important in today's algorithmic trading environment, where sophisticated trading programs sometimes target
visible orders
. Since your potential trade isn't visible to the market, you can manage your risk without telling the world your lowest price.
Important
Stop-loss orders remove emotion from the equation. Once you set it, you've essentially made your exit decision during a calm moment rather than trying to think clearly during market turbulence.
Different Types of Stop-Loss Orders
Standard Stop-Loss Order
The most basic form is the standard stop-loss order, which triggers a market sell order when a stock falls to or below your given stop price.
If you buy a stock at $50 and set a stop-loss at $45, your broker will automatically sell your position at the best market price should the stock drop to or below $45. (In the graphic below, you can click the buttons to flip through the order types to see the differences.)
Understanding Stop-Limit Orders
A stop-limit order adds an extra layer of control by combining two price points: the stop price and the limit price. When the stock reaches your stop price, instead of selling at any available price, the order becomes a limit order that will only execute at the specific limit price you name or better.
Let's say you bought shares of Apple Inc. (
AAPL
) at $225 and want to protect against significant losses, but you also want to ensure you don't sell at an unnecessarily low price if the stock gaps down. You could set a stop-limit order with two prices: a stop price at $165 (which triggers the order) and a limit price at $163 (the lowest price you'll accept).
Here's how it works: If AAPL falls to $165, your stop price triggers the order. However, unlike a regular stop-loss that would sell at any price available, your order will only execute if you can get $163 or better. If AAPL gaps down to $160, your order won't execute because it's below your limit price of $163.
There is a risk to this: if the market is moving fast, that could mean your trade isn't triggered, the stock continues heading south, and you're out of luck selling the shares at a higher price (at least until if and when the price comes back up).
Trailing Stop Order
Trailing stops
are a somewhat more sophisticated version, automatically adjusting the stop price as the stock price moves in your favor. These orders can be set as a fixed dollar amount or a percentage below the market price (typically, it's the latter).
For example, a 10% trailing stop on a stock bought $50 might initially trigger at $45. But if the stock rises to $60, the stop would automatically adjust to $55 or or whatever you wish, protecting more of your gains while allowing for normal price fluctuations.
They are particularly worthwhile during strong upward trends, as they automatically update your stop-loss levels to protect accumulated gains without requiring manual adjustments.
Take-Profit Order (Profit Stop)
While technically not a "stop-loss" order, a
take-profit order
(sometimes called a profit stop) is a logical complement to stop losses. This order type automatically sells your position when the stock reaches a preset profit target.
For example, if you buy a stock at $50 and set a take-profit order at $60, your position will automatically sell when the price reaches $60, securing your 20% profit regardless of whether the stock continues higher or reverses course.
Take-profit orders help enforce discipline to prevent the common mistake of holding a stock too long and watching profits evaporate when the market reverses.
Buy-Stop Order
A
buy stop
can be used in two distinct ways: to enter a new long position when a stock breaks resistance (goes past the upper limit of where it's been trading for a period), or to protect a short position from significant losses. Let's look at both scenarios:
Short-Covering Scenario
Suppose you've shorted Tesla (
TSLA
) at $200, believing its price is sure to fall. To protect against potential losses if the stock rises instead, you might place a buy stop at $220. If Tesla reaches $220, your buy stop automatically triggers, buying shares to cover your short position, limiting your loss to $20 per share.
Breakout Trading Scenario
If a stock is trading at $45 and you believe it will continue rising once it breaks above $50, you could place a buy-stop order at $50. Buy stops are very useful for
momentum traders
since you can program your brokerage system so that when a key resistance level is passed, you can catch the start of a significant upward move.
Fast Fact
Momentum traders focus on stocks showing strong upward or downward price movement, essentially "riding the wave" of market trends. Unlike
value investors
who look for underpriced stocks, momentum traders believe that stocks moving strongly in one direction will tend to continue that way.
Bracket Orders
A
bracket order
combines three coordinated orders:
An entry order (market or limit)
A stop-loss order below the entry price
A take-profit order above the entry price
This creates a "bracket" or zone around your position—you're establishing both your maximum acceptable loss and your profit target simultaneously, essentially creating a complete trading plan in a single order.
Benefits of Using Stop-Loss Orders
Cost-effective
: One of the most attractive aspects of stop-loss orders is their cost. Setting up these orders is typically free, with standard commission fees only applying when the order executes—and many brokerages today offer commission-free trading on most stocks and exchange-traded funds. This makes them an inexpensive or free tool for protecting your portfolio.
Emotional/Psychological Discipline
: Human psychology often works against successful trading. Fear, greed, and hope can lead to poor decision-making. Stop-loss orders remove this emotional component by executing automatically based on criteria you set before any fear, greed, and hope kick in during trading.
Convenience
: Constantly monitoring positions is impractical for most investors. Stop-loss orders provide round-the-clock protection without you having to watch multiple screens like a security guard for a large building.
Managing risk
: Stop-loss orders can help you protect not just one investment but your entire collection of stocks. Just as you wouldn't use the same insurance deductible for a sports car and a family sedan, you can customize stop-loss levels for different stocks. A stable utility company might get a tighter stop-loss since it shouldn't move much, while a volatile tech startup might need more room to bounce around. This flexibility lets you build a safety system that matches your risk tolerance and trading strategy for each stock you own.
Warning
The classic
dead cat bounce
scenario is particularly treacherous when using stop-losses: a stock might trigger your stop-loss during a sudden decline and then stage an equally swift recovery.
Risks of Stop-Loss Orders
Market volatility:
While stop-loss orders can protect against sustained downward trends, they may trigger during temporary price swings. This is particularly relevant in volatile markets where a stock might briefly dip below your stop price before recovering.
Execution risks:
When a stop-loss triggers, it becomes a market order, which means it will execute at the best price. When there's a lot of volatility or not much trading, this price could end up significantly lower than your stop price—a phenomenon known as
slippage
.
Broker restrictions
: Some securities, particularly those trading in extended hours, on foreign exchanges, or over the counter, may not be eligible for stop-loss orders. In addition, brokers have different stop-loss rules that you'll need to check, too.
Tax implications:
Sales triggered by stop losses may have unintended tax consequences, particularly for long-term positions. These automated exits could turn long-term capital gains into short-term gains if triggered before the one-year holding period.
Re-entry risk:
The most challenging aspect of stop-loss orders is managing reentry after being stopped out. You face two risks if you're stopped out and want to get back in: You might have been stopped out of a position that recovers. At that point, you might reenter at a worse price because you misread a temporary bounce as a genuine recovery. Traders can get caught in a cycle of being stopped out at lows, reentering higher, and getting stopped out again.
Tip
In some cases, large market participants may attempt to trigger clusters of stop-loss orders by pushing prices to common stop levels. This practice, known as "
stop hunting
," is prevalent in forex and futures markets where open interest is more visible.

2. Determining Your Stop Price
Choosing where to place your stop-loss order doesn't have to be guesswork. Here are three approaches investors use to determine their exit points:
Chart-Based Method (Technical Analysis)
This approach is like looking for natural "floor" levels in a stock's price. Traders study price charts to find points where a stock has repeatedly bounced back up after falling. They often place stops just below these support levels, as in the chart below:
Investopedia
Many also watch
moving averages
—rolling price averages over periods like 50 or 200 days—and set stops below these key indicators.
Tip
When using technical analysis for stop placement, look at several different time frames. A level that looks like support on a daily chart might show itself to be insignificant on a weekly chart.
Company Assessment (Fundamental Analysis)
This strategy looks at a company's actual value based on its financial health rather than its stock chart. Investors analyze earnings, debt levels, and growth rates to determine a "fair value" for the stock. They also look at any industry- or company-specific risks.
After doing so, they might set stops at a percentage below this calculated value—for instance, 10% under what they determine to be the stock's true value.
Volatility or Price Movement Method
Investors look at the stock's historical
price swings
and set stops based on its normal pattern. For example, if a stock usually moves up or down by $2 per day, they might set stops $4 below their purchase price to avoid being stopped out by normal market movement.

Examples of Effective Stop-Loss Strategies
No matter what type of investor you are, you should be able to easily identify why you own a stock and when to get rid of it. A
value investor's
criteria will be different from the criteria of a
growth
investor, which will be different from the criteria of an
active trader
.
No matter what your strategy is, it will only work if you stick to it. So, if you are a long-term buy-and-hold investor, stop-loss orders are unlikely to be used.
Still, many investors fall somewhere between ultra-long-term holders and active day traders, so stop-loss orders can serve as valuable guardrails to enforce disciplined exits. The key is aligning your stop-loss approach with your investment philosophy and risk tolerance rather than adopting a one-size-fits-all approach.
Below are some hypothetical examples of how investors might use stop-loss orders based on the types of stocks involved or their overall strategy:
Growth Stock Setup
Growth investors focus on companies with rapid revenue and earnings expansion, like technology firms. These stocks often have bigger price swings, requiring wider stops to avoid premature exits. A growth investor might buy a promising software company at $100 per share and set their initial stop at $92, allowing for normal volatility while protecting against serious declines.
Entry: $100
Initial stop: 7% to 8% below entry ($92 to $93)
Trailing stop: 10% to 12% below highs
Volume requirement: Above the average daily volume
Value Stock Setup
Value investors hunt for bargain-priced stocks trading below their perceived worth, such as established financial or industrial companies. These typically stable stocks permit tighter stops. For instance, when buying a banking stock at $50, a value investor might place their stop at $47, as these mature companies usually have less volatile prices.
Entry: $50
Initial stop: 5% to 6% below entry ($47 to $47.50)
Trailing stop: 8% below highs
Support requirement: Stop placed below the major support level
Volatile Stock Setup
Momentum investors chase stocks showing strong upward price movement, like recent
initial public offerings
or trending sectors. These require the widest stops because of their potential for dramatic price swings. An investor buying a hot electric vehicle stock at $25 might set their stop at $21.25 to accommodate the stock's larger daily moves while protecting their downside.
Entry: $25
Initial stop: 12% to 15% below entry ($21.25 to $22)
Trailing Stop: 15% to 20% below highs
Volume requirement: Twice the average daily volume
Common Pitfalls to Avoid With Stop-Loss Orders
1. Confusing Risk Tolerance for Market Volatility
For newer investors, the most frequent pitfall while using stop-loss orders comes from a fundamental misunderstanding of market volatility. Traders might set their stops based on their risk tolerance rather than the stock's actual trading behavior. So, they might use a tight 5% stop on a stock that routinely experiences 7% intraday swings, essentially guaranteeing they'll be stopped out by normal market shifts.
This tendency to focus on personal comfort over the market's real trading habits can lead to "death by a thousand cuts"—many small losses that could have been avoided with better-placed stops.
2. Putting Stop-Losses in Motion
Another critical error is when traders fall into the trap of adjusting stops in the wrong direction. While it's psychologically challenging to accept a loss, moving a stop-loss lower to avoid taking a hit violates the very purpose of having stop losses in the first place.
When you start moving stops with the market, you've transformed a tool for managing risk into one where you might amplify it. This behavior can kickstart major losses and can turn manageable setbacks into account-threatening disasters.
3. Not Adapting to Changing Conditions
Another mistake is not adjusting stop-losses at all and failing to adjust them for changing market conditions. Markets move through distinct phases (i.e., trending, ranging, volatile, and calm), each requiring different approaches to where you place a stop-loss.
Your stop strategy should breathe with the market: widening during volatile periods and tightening during calmer ones. It's about finding the sweet spot between protecting your assets and enabling the stock some breathing room in the current market environment. Again, you don't want to be moving stop losses and hoping for a price recovery, but you should shift at times to better capture gains as the size of price swings changes.

Day trades:
Tighter stops (less than 1% to 2%)
Swing trades: Medium stops (4% to 8%)
Position trades: Wider stops (8% to 15%)
Long-term investments: Consider using moving averages or quarterly lows
What Are The Best Practices For Setting Stop-Loss Percentages?
The best stop-loss percentage varies by individual preference, security type, and market context rather than following a one-size-fits-all approach. For instance, with stable blue chip stocks, stops might range from 5% to 8% below the purchase price, while more volatile growth stocks could warrant setting stops 10% to 15% below the price.
The key is matching the stop percentage to the security's price action as well as your risk tolerance, investment horizon,
trading strategy
, and risk tolerance.
What Are The Common Mistakes Investors Make With Stop-Loss Orders?
The most damaging mistake is placing stops based on arbitrary percentages rather than market context and technical levels. Other critical errors include widening stops to avoid taking losses, failing to adjust stops during significant market regime changes, and not accounting for earnings announcements or other events known to spark volatility.
How Do Stop-Loss Orders Affect Long-Term Investment Strategies?
Long-term investors often use wider or trailing stops to avoid being shaken out of positions during normal market fluctuations. Some adopt a scaled approach, using 15% to 20% stops for core positions while employing tighter stops of 8% to 12% on shorter-term holdings. Others prefer using long-term moving averages, such as the 200-day line, for dynamic stop levels rather than fixed percentages for long-term positions.
Can Stop-Loss Orders Be Used In All Types Of Markets?
While stop-loss orders can, in theory, be implemented in any market, their effectiveness will vary. They tend to work best in trending markets with normal volatility levels. During highly volatile periods or market crashes, stops may execute at prices far below their triggers because of gaps and
liquidity
issues.
Can a Stop-Loss Trigger a Buy Order?
Yes, stop-losses can also be used for placing orders (known as a buy stop). It allows an investor to automatically buy a security once it reaches a certain price. This type of order can be useful for investors who want to enter a position at a specific price point.
The Bottom Line
A stop-loss order is a straightforward tool that can offer significant advantages when used effectively. Whether to prevent
excessive losses
or to lock in profits, almost all investing styles can benefit from its use. A stop-loss is like an insurance policy: You hope you never have to use it, but it's good to know you have the protection there.
The key to success lies in understanding their capabilities and limitations and applying them carefully to fit your broader investment strategy. As markets evolve and your experience grows, your approach to using stop-loss orders should also adapt. Regularly reviewing and adjusting your stop-loss strategy will ensure it continues to serve you well while adjusting to changing market conditions.
